# What the science says about cats and screens

Status: product research baseline, rewritten for owners and curators
Search closed: 29 July 2026
Scope: domestic-cat content curation, playback, audio, session design, device setup, and welfare safeguards

This page is the plain-language edition of Catflix's research baseline. It
replaces the earlier technical edition, "Scientific foundation for Catflix
curation", which remains in the repository's git history. It follows that
edition section by section and keeps every number, hedge, and limitation.
Where it adds an everyday example or a definition, that addition is
explanation, not new evidence. Sentences marked `[Research review, <section>]`
point back to the matching section of the earlier edition, and evidence IDs
such as `VID-01` are the study labels used throughout the evidence ledger
(`docs/research/evidence-ledger.csv` in the repository), the spreadsheet that
records each source study one row at a time. This review is also what
Catflix's product principles (`PRODUCT.md` in the repository) are built on.

## In brief

Catflix's five scenes are built on real findings about what cats can see and
hear, and on repeated evidence that cats pay attention to certain kinds of
on-screen movement. That is worth taking seriously. But it is not the same as
proof that cats enjoy watching, or that watching benefits them. A cat that
looks, tracks, or pounces at a screen is demonstrating **attention** — not
necessarily **preference** (a choice it would make again given other
options) or **welfare** (how it fares over time). This review keeps those
ideas separate throughout, and so should you.

Evidence that comes directly from cats watching screens is thin: one shelter
trial plus four other screen or visual-media experiments, out of 60 sources
in total `[Research review, Executive conclusion]`. That thinness is exactly
why Catflix is cautious: sessions are short and always optional, sound starts
off, clips rotate instead of intensifying, and you are asked to watch your
own cat and stop if something looks wrong, rather than trust a general rule.

**What to do with this page:** read "Four words we keep apart" and "What
Catflix does because of this" for the short version. Read "What the studies
show, topic by topic" if you want the reasoning. Read "Safe setup and when to
stop" and "Watching your cat" before you run a session. The rest — methods,
the full source list, and the glossary — is there so any claim on this page
can be checked.

## Four words we keep apart

The review uses four words in a deliberately narrow way, and keeps them
separate through every section `[Research review, Executive conclusion]`:

- **Sensory capacity** — what a cat *can detect* (sensory capacity): what a
  cat can physically detect or tell apart, under stated test conditions. For
  example, a lab test can show a cat can tell two colors apart under careful
  lighting. That tells us the cat's eyes and brain are capable of it — nothing
  about whether the cat cares.
- **Attention**: looking, orienting, tracking, approaching. Attention is an
  orientation response — a look, a turned ear, a followed gaze, a step
  toward something — caused by a stimulus. A cat can pay close attention to
  something it finds alarming, confusing, or simply new, not only to
  something it likes.
- **Preference**: a voluntary choice between options. Preference is a
  voluntary choice between two or more things that are actually available,
  ideally shown more than once and with a real option to walk away. Looking
  at one thing does not tell you the cat would choose it over another thing,
  or over nothing.
- **Welfare**: how the cat fares beyond the moment. Welfare covers positive,
  neutral, frustrating, stressful, or physically risky outcomes, judged
  beyond the moment of looking. A cat can attend closely to something for a
  few seconds and be no better or worse off for it — or worse off, if the
  thing turns out to be startling.

Putting it together: a cat can detect a moving light on a screen (capacity),
turn and watch it move (attention), without that telling us whether the cat
would choose to watch it again given other options (preference), or whether
repeated viewing is good, neutral, or bad for that cat over time (welfare).
Catflix's rules, later on this page, try never to let evidence of one of
these four things stand in for another.

## How much evidence there is

The evidence ledger holds 60 unique peer-reviewed sources: 57 original
empirical studies and 3 scholarly reviews. Each source has one primary
subject area and is counted once `[Research review, Executive conclusion]`.

Most of the corpus is domestic-cat behavioral, field, developmental, or
clinical research, plus feline neurophysiology (the study of how the nervous
system works, here usually recording directly from nerves or brain
structures) covering questions of raw capacity.

Evidence that comes directly from a cat looking at a display is much
smaller: **one television-enrichment trial plus four screen or visual-media
experiments**, and only the television trial measured behavior in a setting
meant to enrich a cat's environment. That is not enough evidence to call
watching a cat video a welfare intervention (something shown to actively
improve a cat's wellbeing) `[Research review, Executive conclusion]`.

## What Catflix does because of this

The Research review's decision table turns the evidence into ten concrete
product decisions. Each one names its supporting evidence, states how
confident the review is, says when it applies, says what it does **not**
prove, and says how a household could check it. The confidence words below
(moderate, low, high, provisional) are the review's own labels, kept exactly
`[Research review, Executive decision table]`.

**1. Curate moving subjects that stand out clearly from their background, and
record contrast as metadata.**
Evidence: COL-05, COL-07, SPA-03, SPA-04, SPA-07 — how well a cat can detect
something changes with its size, distance, brightness, contrast, and timing.
Confidence: **Moderate**. Applies to any programme meant to be visible at a
known device size and roughly known viewing distance. This does **not**
mean there is one universal best color, or that high contrast is always
preferred. To check at home: watch a cat's orientation and tracking across
matched clips that differ mainly in how clearly the subject stands out from
its background (figure-ground separation).

**2. Prefer believable, prey-like or object-like movement paths over motion
added purely to look intense.**
Evidence: MOT-02, MOT-07, VID-01, VID-02, VID-03. Confidence: **Moderate for
attention; low for welfare**. Applies to short, supervised sessions with
uncluttered backgrounds and an easy way for the cat to leave. This does
**not** mean longer looking equals enjoyment, hunting satisfaction, or a
welfare benefit. To check at home: compare matched straight-line,
stop-and-start, and relationship-based movement paths for the same cat, and
note when the cat disengages.

**3. Treat novelty as something that gets used up, and rotate clips instead
of making them faster or busier.**
Evidence: HUN-05 and VID-01 both show response declining with repetition;
HUN-07 shows preferences vary a lot between individual cats. Confidence:
**Moderate**. Applies to repeated household sessions using clips that can be
told apart by ID. This does **not** mean one instance of a cat disengaging
proves boredom, and it is not a license to chase the longest possible
uninterrupted session. To check at home: bring a clip back after a rest
period and note whether the cat returns to it on its own, without being
prompted.

**4. Keep sound optional, occasional rather than constant, matched to what's
on screen, and as quiet as possible while still producing ordinary
orientation, without distress.**
Evidence: AUD-02, AUD-05, AUD-06, AUD-07, AUD-08; WEL-05, WEL-06. Confidence:
**Moderate for localization and orientation; low for video welfare**. Applies
when speakers are stable, the cat can leave, playback starts quietly, and
there's no known sound sensitivity. This does **not** mean that a cat being
able to hear a very high-pitched (ultrasonic — above the range of human
hearing, a general fact about sound, not a specific research finding) sound
makes that sound something the cat would want, and ear or head movement is
not evidence of enjoyment. To check at home: compare matched
sound-on/sound-off sessions and stop at any startle, avoidance, freezing, or
ongoing agitation.

**5. Publish no exact minimum screen refresh-rate rule. Use modern displays
with no visible stutter or flicker under the real brightness and motion
being shown.**
Evidence: SPA-01, SPA-03, SPA-04, SPA-05. Confidence: **High that how
visible flicker is depends on context; low for turning that into a single
product number**. Applies to testing each device Catflix actually supports,
at its real brightness, file format, frame timing, and clip motion. This
does **not** mean a flicker speed measured in a 1970s lab — the flicker speed
at which flicker stops being visible ran from 40 to 55 Hz in that study —
is a universal frame-rate specification for consumer screens. To check at
home: watch high-motion clips on each supported device and include a
household check for visible stutter or flicker.

**6. Default to short, voluntary, supervised sessions with an obvious way to
leave.**
Evidence: VID-01 shows low average screen attention that fell across
exposure; WEL-01 through WEL-08 show that stress signs and how individual
cats cope both vary. Confidence: **Moderate as a precaution; low as a claim
that sessions actually do something beneficial**. Applies when the device is
secured, the cat is healthy enough for normal play, and a person can stop
playback. This does **not** mean a single universal session length can be
set from the three-hour shelter protocol described below. To check at home:
the household protocol records how long it takes a cat to disengage,
whether it returns on its own, and what happens afterward — described in
words, not scored as a number.

**7. Offer a safe way to physically "catch" something after repeated screen
strikes or searching behind the screen.**
Evidence: HUN-01, HUN-04, HUN-05, HUN-08; VID-01. Confidence: **Provisional**
(an early, unconfirmed step, not an established rule). Applies when a cat
repeatedly pounces, paws at the screen, or keeps searching after an
on-screen target disappears. This does **not** mean every video without a
"catch" causes frustration, or that a toy automatically fixes it if it does.
To check at home: compare what a cat does after a session with, versus
without, a brief physical play opportunity — without forcing the cat to
take part.

**8. Use descriptive notes for Arri, Ozzy, and Mika — the cats behind
Catflix's own curation — never automated cat profiles or preference scores.**
Evidence: HUN-06, HUN-07, IND-02, IND-03 show real variation between
individual cats, but also show the limits of how that variation is
measured. Confidence: **High for this being the right governance rule;
moderate for how much individual cats actually vary**. Applies as long as
notes stay dated, descriptive, and tied to one clip and one context. This
does **not** mean household notes can establish a stable personality, a
breed-wide rule, a diagnosis, or one universal preference. To check at home:
repeat matched observations from time to time, and keep contradictory
results rather than averaging them into a single score.

**9. Leave out or add extra supervision to rapidly flashing edits, unstable
devices, loud or spatially confusing sound, and clips that repeatedly cause
collisions or distressed searching.**
Evidence: SPA-01, AUD-02, AUD-05, WEL-05, WEL-06; the review is explicit that
physical risk here is a precautionary product decision, not something a
video-effect study has actually tested. Confidence: **Moderate precautionary
confidence**. Applies to all household playback, with stricter review for
kittens, older cats, and cats with visual, hearing, neurological, pain, or
anxiety concerns. This does **not** mean the research literature supplies
exact clinical safety limits for flashing, volume, or how long a session
should run. To check at home: an owner review, a device-stability check,
and veterinary advice whenever health or neurological vulnerability is a
possibility.

**10. Label how confident every piece of content-level evidence is, and keep
attention evidence visibly separate from preference and welfare evidence.**
Evidence: the entire ledger, especially VID-01 through VID-05 and WEL-01
through WEL-08. Confidence: **High**. Applies to editorial curation,
household notes, product copy, and any future testing. This does **not**
mean "watched," "tracked," or "pounced" may ever be turned into "liked,"
"beneficial," or "safe" without evidence that actually measures that
specific thing. To check at home: an editorial check of every public claim
against the evidence ledger's IDs.

**No recommendation here is labelled strong.** The most defensible rules are
moderate, conditional design choices, or high-confidence boundaries on what
may honestly be claimed. That follows a rule set before the review began: a
*strong* product recommendation would need two independent studies, at least
one of them an awake behavioral study — a bar none of these ten decisions
clears on its own `[Research review, Executive decision table]`.

## What the studies show, topic by topic

### Legibility, not a "best color"

Behavioral studies show cats can tell colors apart when brightness cues are
controlled for (COL-01, COL-02, COL-05, COL-07, COL-08). Studies of the
nervous system (neurophysiology — the study of how nerves and the brain
work, usually by recording directly from them) show the biological wiring
that could support that kind of discrimination (COL-03, COL-04, COL-06), but
a nerve responding is not an awake choice, and it says nothing about
enjoyment.

How big, what shape, how far away, how bright, and how much contrast a
stimulus has all change how well a cat can detect it (COL-05, COL-07,
SPA-04, SPA-07). That's why Catflix records apparent size from where the cat
sits (visual angle) and contrast descriptors rather than declaring one hue a
cat "preference." "Blue and yellow are best for cats" is too simple a claim:
whether something is easy to detect depends on the subject, the background,
the lighting, the device, the distance, and the task together — not on a
color swatch by itself `[Research review, Visual legibility, not a "best
color"]`.

### Screens and flicker

The behavioral temporal-modulation study used **three** trained cats and
found the flicker speed at which flicker stops being visible (its critical
flicker fusion, or CFF) ran from **40 to 55 Hz under its tested conditions**
(SPA-01). That number is real, useful evidence that timing matters to a
cat's vision — but it is *not* a minimum display refresh rate. Frame rate,
panel refresh, how a screen holds a frame, brightness, how deep the light
modulation is, video compression, camera shutter speed, how fast something
is moving, and viewing distance are all different things and don't stand in
for one another.

Similarly, estimates of how sharp feline vision is shift depending on how
the test was run. A study of **two** cats under unusually bright, favorable
conditions found sharper detail vision (grating acuity — how fine a striped
pattern a cat can still tell apart from plain gray) than many earlier
studies (SPA-07), while a separate study (SPA-04) shows contrast sensitivity
shifting with brightness. Catflix responds by testing on the actual
supported device and recording apparent object size, rather than promising
a resolution or refresh-rate number detached from real viewing conditions
`[Research review, Spatial and temporal rendering]`.

### Motion, trajectory, and occlusion

Cats can tell different kinds of motion apart in controlled tasks and are
sensitive to how objects move relative to each other (MOT-02, MOT-07,
VID-02, VID-03). They also use spatial and cross-sense information about
objects that have gone out of sight (occlusion — passing behind something)
(MOT-01, MOT-03, MOT-04, MOT-06). Together, these findings support
believable movement paths, occlusion and reappearance a cat can follow, and
uncluttered backgrounds as things worth curating for.

They do **not** show that faster movement is always better. A moving target
can become too fast to usefully track, get visually lost against a busy
background, or lead to a cat repeatedly running into the screen. Catflix's
approach is to keep clips with a range of speeds and stop-and-start motion,
and to learn only from descriptive household notes, not from chasing more
intense motion `[Research review, Motion, trajectory, and occlusion]`.

### Sound

Cats hear and can locate a wide range of sounds, with how well they do so
depending on pitch, sound bandwidth, and where the sound is coming from
(AUD-01 through AUD-04). Familiar speech can make a cat turn an ear or the
head (AUD-06, AUD-07), and in one study, music written to match a cat's own
vocal range and tempo led to more approaching or interacting than ordinary
human music (AUD-08). Where a cat looks shifts differently for sounds than
for sights (AUD-05).

The design choice this supports is sound that's optional, occasional, and
matched to what's on screen (sound that seems to come from the thing on
screen — diegetic, or source-coherent, sound). The leap that is *not*
supported is adding ultrasonic content (sound above the range of human
hearing — this particular definition is general knowledge, not a specific
research finding) or a "scientifically tuned" sound preset. Being able to
hear something is not evidence that a cat wants to hear it, benefits from
it, or that it's safe, and most household speakers can't reproduce very
high-pitched content accurately anyway. A sound that appears to come from
something on screen also should not drift off to the wrong side of the room
`[Research review, Audio and screen-speaker coherence]`.

### Play and hunting

Object play overlaps with hunting behavior, but it isn't simply a response
to hunger (HUN-01, HUN-04). Repeating the same thing reduces play, and
changing the stimulus can bring the response back (HUN-05) — this pattern of
"responding less to something repeated" is called habituation. How much
cats want to play varies a great deal between individuals (HUN-06, HUN-07),
and regular physical object play with a cat can change how much wildlife it
brings home (HUN-08).

These findings support rotating clips (clip families — clips that share a
subject, background, motion, and sound, called `novelty_family` in
Catflix's own records) and optionally moving to physical play. They do
**not** prove that video without a "catch" at the end frustrates every cat,
or that adding an on-screen catch gives a cat what a real physical capture
would. A cat repeatedly hitting the screen, searching behind it, being
unable to settle, or redirecting aggression are signals to stop or
reconsider a clip at home — not goals to aim for `[Research review, Hunting,
play, novelty, and capture]`.

### The direct screen studies

This is the small core of evidence that comes from cats actually looking at
screens. **VID-01** randomly assigned **125 shelter cats** across what the
review describes as **five conditions**; across the conditions where a
television was present, cats looked at the screen in just **6.10% of
observations** — about 6 of every 100 sampled moments, using a method called
scan sampling (checking and recording what an animal is doing at set
intervals, rather than watching continuously). Moving content, whether it
showed animals or objects, drew more attention than clips of humans or a
blank screen, and attention fell off over repeated days of exposure. This
design supports a conclusion about attention and habituation. It does
**not** establish that shelter cats — or any cats — preferred the television,
felt good about it, or benefited from it over time.

> **Note:** the Research review describes VID-01 as having "five conditions,"
> while the evidence ledger lists four stimulus types shown to the cats:
> blank screen, humans, inanimate movement, and animate (prey-like)
> movement. A fifth, no-television control condition would explain the
> difference, and the review's own phrase "television-present conditions"
> hints at one — but neither document actually states that a fifth condition
> existed. This rewrite leaves the discrepancy exactly as it stands in the
> source rather than guessing which number is right.

The remaining screen studies confirm that cats can respond to moving lights,
random moving dots, apparent motion across a flat surface, and animated
relationships between objects (VID-02 through VID-05, and MOT-07). Most of
these studies use very small or specially selected groups of cats, brief
exposure, trained responses, food or attention deprivation, invasive
recording methods, video clips posted online by owners, or measures that
have nothing to do with welfare. Catflix's own language sticks to
"screen-visible and attention-relevant," not "proven enriching" `[Research
review, Direct display evidence]`.

### Welfare and vulnerable cats

Feline stress can show up in activity level, posture, eating, litter-box use,
signs of feeling unwell, the stress hormone cortisol, and social behavior —
and these measures don't always agree with each other (WEL-01 through
WEL-08). Findings from shelters, catteries, hospitals, labs, and people's own
homes can't be assumed to transfer from one setting to another without
qualification. There is no peer-reviewed feline study of video specifically
that sets a maximum safe session length, a safe sound level, a safe flash
rate, or a diagnostic stop score.

Health and age change how behavior should be read. Pain, reduced sensory
ability, anxiety, and cognitive dysfunction (a decline in mental function,
similar in concept to dementia) can all change how a cat orients, vocalizes,
avoids things, or recovers afterward (IND-04 through IND-07). A senior cat,
or one with neurological vulnerability, should get quieter, simpler,
more closely supervised sessions, with a veterinarian consulted if behavior
changes are new or worrying. Catflix must never present playback as
treatment `[Research review, Welfare and vulnerable cats]`.

## Safe setup and when to stop

**Current implementation note:** Catflix generates each scene's sound itself,
inside the browser, at the moment it's played (using the Web Audio API — the
browser's built-in sound-generation tool). No recording is bundled with the
app or streamed from anywhere. The audio rules below govern that
synthesized sound `[Research review, Playback and safety principles]`.

1. Playback is always optional for the cat. Never place or hold a cat in
   front of a screen. One study (MOT-07) shows why being physically handled
   during testing limits how much a result can be said to reflect a cat's
   own voluntary preference.
2. Secure televisions and tablets against tipping, sliding, claw damage,
   cable access, and falls before playback. This precaution follows
   screen-directed reaching and striking seen in VID-01, VID-02, and VID-04;
   no study of device injury sets an exact safety threshold.
3. Begin without sound, or at the household's quietest ordinary level.
   Increase only when calm behavior continues; never use ultrasonic content.
   See AUD-01, AUD-02, WEL-05, and WEL-06.

   > **Note:** this principle assumes sound can be gradually increased if a
   > cat stays calm. Catflix's implementation currently uses one fixed,
   > quiet, synthesized sound level rather than a range. The source material
   > doesn't specify exactly how an owner should "increase" the level — in
   > practice, this would mean adjusting the device's own volume control.
   > This rewrite flags that gap rather than resolving it.

4. Provide a clear, unobstructed way for the cat to leave, and don't restart
   playback just to get its attention back. See HUN-05 and VID-01.
5. Stop immediately after a collision, repeated hard strikes at the screen,
   marked startle, freezing, hiding, distressed vocalizing, redirected
   aggression, ongoing searching behind the screen, loss of balance, or any
   behavior the observer considers abnormal. This is a conservative,
   precautionary stop rule informed by VID-01, WEL-05, and WEL-06 — it is
   **not** a validated feline-video scale.
6. Treat ordinary orientation, tracking, pouncing, disengaging, and later
   returning as separate, individually recorded observations. None of them
   alone is a welfare verdict. See VID-01, AUD-06, and AUD-07.
7. For kittens, older cats, or cats with visual, hearing, neurological,
   pain, anxiety, or cognitive concerns, use simpler and quieter content
   with active supervision. Get veterinary advice for anything new or
   persistent. See IND-01 and IND-04 through IND-07.
8. Never use a toy or food to force a cat toward the screen. A brief,
   voluntary physical play opportunity may follow repeated predatory
   interaction with the screen, but its effect should be observed, not
   assumed. See HUN-01, HUN-04, HUN-05, and HUN-08.
9. Don't loop a clip forever. Rotating clips is preferable to making the
   motion, sound, or editing more intense to hold attention. See HUN-05 and
   VID-01.
10. Motion and accessibility requirements for the human-facing interface are
    a separate matter from the feline content curation rules above.

## Watching your cat: the household observation protocol

This is a lightweight way for Catflix's curator to check assumptions about
Arri, Ozzy, and Mika at home. **It is not an experiment, a diagnosis, a
biometric system, or an automated profile** `[Research review, Household
observation protocol for Arri, Ozzy, and Mika]`.

### Setup

- Test one cat at a time, and only when that cat approaches or stays in the
  room voluntarily.
- Secure the device, cables, and nearby objects. Keep the cat's usual exit
  route open.
- Write down which cat, the date and time, the clip's ID and revision, the
  device, roughly how far away the cat is, the room's lighting, whether
  sound is on, what the cat recently ate or played, and who is observing.
- Where possible, compare two versions that differ in one main way — for
  example contrast, movement path, sound, or clip family — rather than many
  things at once.
- Never lure, place, restrain, wake, or repeatedly call the cat toward the
  screen `[Research review, Setup]`.

### Event vocabulary

The protocol uses a fixed set of words for what gets written down, so notes
stay comparable over time:

| Event | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| `voluntary_approach` | The cat moves toward the playback area on its own, without being lured or handled. |
| `orientation` | The head, ears, or eyes turn toward the screen or speaker. The observer records which, when it's clear. |
| `tracking` | The head or gaze follows a visible object's path for more than a moment. |
| `pouncing` | A deliberate leap, paw strike, or capture-like movement toward the display area. Contact and force are described in words, not scored. |
| `disengagement` | The cat looks away, leaves, grooms, rests, or turns to something else. This is not recorded as a failure. |
| `re_engagement` | The cat's attention returns after at least 10 seconds of disengagement, without a person prompting it. That 10-second interval is a rule the observers use for consistency — it is not a biological threshold. |
| `post_session_behavior` | For the next few ordinary minutes, the observer notes whether the cat seems settled, play-seeking, searching, hiding, agitated, aggressive, vocal, or something else — described, not scored as a mood. |

`[Research review, Event vocabulary]`

### Session note

Each session gets a plain note: roughly when each event happened, why
playback ended, any safety event, and one plain-language observation. The
review's own example: *"Mika approached during the second bird entrance,
tracked two left-to-right passes, tapped the lower bezel once, then left and
groomed. No return before playback ended."* Notes never say something like
"Mika loved birds."

A comparison is repeated on more than one day before it becomes a curator
note. Sessions where nothing happened, or where two sessions contradict each
other, are kept rather than smoothed away. A referee note may say "tracked
in two quiet evening observations" or "no approach observed" — never
"favorite," "addicted," "calming," or "therapeutic," unless there is
evidence that actually measures that specific thing `[Research review,
Session note]`.

## What we may and may not say

- **Permitted:** "Includes intermittent small-animal movement on a
  low-complexity background; evidence supports visibility and attention
  relevance."
- **Permitted:** "Arri tracked this revision in two supervised household
  observations."
- **Not permitted:** "Scientifically proven to entertain cats."
- **Not permitted:** "Optimized for feline color vision," unless the exact
  contrast, device, and validation method are stated.
- **Not permitted:** "60 Hz is required for cats," "ultrasonic enrichment,"
  "anti-anxiety video," or "prevents cognitive decline."
- **Not permitted:** breed-specific modes, claims of a fixed personality
  profile, or ranking clips by how long they hold uninterrupted attention.

`[Research review, Product claim rules]`

## What we don't know yet

The review lists ten specific gaps in the evidence, kept here in full
`[Research review, Limitations and research gaps]`:

1. Direct evidence testing television or tablet enrichment in domestic cats
   is extremely sparse. The largest direct trial used shelter cats, CRT-era
   video (video shot or displayed on an old cathode-ray-tube screen, an
   older display technology with different flicker and motion behavior than
   modern screens), long exposure blocks, and scan-sampled looking behavior.
2. Many of the foundational vision and hearing studies use just two to five
   specially trained cats. Several of the studies of the nervous system are
   invasive or done under anesthesia (with the animal sedated and
   unconscious). These establish biological mechanisms or thresholds, not
   spontaneous choices cats make in a home.
3. A single device changes apparent size, brightness, contrast, how motion
   is rendered, delay, video compression, and speaker placement all at
   once. A result from one laboratory screen does not add up to a
   prescription that works for every device.
4. Orientation and looking time are often the only measurements a study can
   practically take. They can signal novelty, surprise, alertness, or
   concern just as easily as attraction. They are not interchangeable with
   positive feeling.
5. Findings from shelters, catteries, colonies, veterinary clinics, and
   laboratories may not transfer to a familiar home. Surveys of cat owners
   add their own biases: what people remember, who volunteers to answer,
   and how owners interpret their cats' behavior.
6. Studies built from online videos or community science carry risks around
   who chooses to participate, who drops out, and pseudoreplication
   (treating many repeated observations of very few individual animals or
   clips as if they were many independent data points). A popular online
   clip is not a random sample of cats or of viewing conditions.
7. No study in this review directly tests whether an on-screen chase that
   never resolves causes frustration, whether a chance at physical capture
   afterward fixes that, or what the best session length would be.
8. No study in this review sets validated safety limits for a cat video's
   flash rate, sound pressure level, motion intensity, how fast cuts happen,
   or total exposure. Any exact number Catflix might publish here would
   overstate what the evidence actually shows.
9. The literature on aging and clinical health supports caution, but does
   not support turning Catflix into a diagnostic tool. New disorientation,
   vocalizing, startling, avoidance, or a change in movement belongs with a
   veterinarian.
10. Several older studies only have their abstracts available through
    stable indexes, not the full text. Sample sizes marked `NR` (not
    reported in the indexed record available to the reviewers — not
    necessarily unreported in the original paper) would need the full text
    recovered before they could be used in any numerical calculation.

## What would come next

The household protocol above is meant to test Catflix's own implementation
assumptions, not to "prove" the research literature one way or the other.
The most useful comparisons to run first are matched contrast, how
intermittent the motion is, sound on versus off, and repeated versus rotated
clips. Before publishing any clinical- or safety-facing claim, a veterinarian
or feline-welfare specialist should review the language and its limits. A
later, more formal study would need to be preregistered (its design and
analysis plan written down and filed before data collection starts, so
results can't be reshaped afterward), use counterbalancing (systematically
varying the order conditions are shown in, so order itself doesn't bias the
result) within each cat, use blinded coding (having someone score the
video who doesn't know which condition they're looking at, so their
expectations can't influence the score), use a design where the cat makes a
real voluntary choice, follow cats over time, define explicit welfare
outcomes, measure the actual device with photometry (instrument
measurement of light output, rather than an estimate) and acoustic
measurement, and use a sample of cats larger than just Arri, Ozzy, and Mika
`[Research review, Next research step]`.

## For editors: the curation record

Every curated programme or clip carries a stable `content_id`, a `revision`
number, and the editorial record below. Every field is an observation or a
measurement about the content and the setting it's meant for — never a
prediction about how a particular cat will feel `[Research review, Curation
metadata contract]`.

| Field | Required representation | Plain-language explanation | Evidence and rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `subject_class` | Controlled term plus free-text detail: bird, small mammal, fish, insect, string/object, abstract motion, conspecific, human, mixed, unknown. | What kind of thing is on screen. | HUN-01, HUN-06, VID-01. Never call a class universally preferred. |
| `apparent_object_size` | Object height/width as percentage of frame; intended viewing-distance band; computed approximate visual-angle band when device size is known. | How big the subject looks from roughly where the cat will sit — its apparent size. | COL-05, COL-07, SPA-07. Never store pixels alone as apparent size. |
| `luminance_contrast` | Foreground/background relative luminance class: low, medium, high; measurement method or editorial basis; dark-scene flag. | How much the subject stands out from its background in plain brightness. | SPA-04, SPA-07. A class is not a preference claim. |
| `chromatic_contrast` | Foreground and background color descriptors plus confidence that they remain separable without hue alone. | How much the subject stands out from its background in color, separate from brightness. | COL-01 through COL-08. No universal "cat color" label. |
| `motion` | Median and peak apparent speed class; trajectory type; intermittency; direction-change rate; entrance/exit edge; acceleration; occlusion frequency and duration. | How the subject moves: how fast, how steadily, and whether it passes behind anything. | MOT-02, MOT-07, VID-01 through VID-03. Do not optimize a single intensity score. |
| `background_complexity` | Low, medium, high, with edge density or editorial rubric and camouflage/visual-clutter note. | How busy or cluttered the background is. | SPA-03 through SPA-05, VID-03. Keep the method with the value. |
| `audio_source` | Diegetic subject, ambience, music, human voice, synthetic effect, none, unknown. | Where the sound is supposed to be coming from. | AUD-05 through AUD-08. |
| `audio_level` | Mastering level relative to programme baseline; quiet-start flag; no absolute safe feline value claimed. | How loud the sound is, relative to Catflix's own baseline — not a claim about a "safe" volume for cats. | AUD-01, WEL-05, WEL-06. Household device volume remains contextual. |
| `audio_bandwidth` | Measured encoded frequency band and codec; ultrasonic-marketing flag must be false for approved content. | The actual pitch range the audio file contains; approved content is never marketed as ultrasonic. | AUD-01, AUD-02. Audibility is not desirability. |
| `audio_intermittency` | Continuous, periodic, event-linked, sparse; onset count per minute. | How often sound starts and stops, rather than playing continuously. | AUD-05 through AUD-08. Sudden-onset review required. |
| `screen_speaker_coherence` | Coherent, ambiguous, intentionally non-diegetic, or incoherent; spatial rationale. | Whether the sound seems to come from the right place relative to what's on screen. | AUD-02 through AUD-05. |
| `novelty_family` | Reusable family ID for shared subject, background, trajectory, and soundtrack; revision relationship. | The clip family this clip belongs to — clips that share a subject, background, motion, and sound. | HUN-05, VID-01. Enables rotation without claiming boredom. |
| `expected_habituation` | Unknown, low-evidence, or observed-in-household; never a fixed countdown. | Whether, and how, this clip is expected to get a weaker response with repeat viewing (habituation) — recorded as a category, never a scheduled countdown. | HUN-05, VID-01. Store observation dates. |
| `session_context` | Intended device class, distance band, light context, quiet-start requirement, voluntary exit, and human presence. | The setting this clip is meant to be watched in. | SPA-01, SPA-04, WEL-01 through WEL-08. |
| `supervision` | Standard, active, or do-not-play; rationale. | How closely a person should be watching during this clip. | Physical-risk precaution plus WEL evidence. |
| `welfare_risk_flags` | Flash/judder, loud or startling onset, repeated no-capture loop, screen-edge exit, conspecific distress sound, collision risk, behind-screen search, unknown. | Known reasons this clip might need extra care — a flag, not a diagnosis. | Precautionary; no flag is a diagnosis. |
| `physical_risk_flags` | Unstable device, reachable cables, breakable screen, sharp stand, tip risk, blocked exit, elevated fall risk. | Physical hazards in the playback setup itself, separate from the video content. | Product safety constraint; verify in each household. |
| `evidence_confidence` | Direct-moderate, direct-low, supporting, indirect-capacity, or unknown; list evidence IDs and endpoint type. | How strong, and what kind of, evidence backs this record — kept separate for capacity, attention, preference, and welfare. | Keeps capacity, attention, preference, and welfare separate. |
| `referee_notes` | Separate arrays for Arri, Ozzy, and Mika; date, clip revision, context, observed behavior, and observer. | The dated, descriptive household notes for each of the three cats. | Descriptive household validation only; no aggregate cat score. |

An approved record must also state `evidence_endpoint`: `capacity`,
`attention`, `preference`, `welfare`, or a combination, each with its own
citation. If a rule is supported only by physiology (biological mechanism
evidence, not behavior), `indirect-capacity` is mandatory.

## How this review was done

### The question

What peer-reviewed evidence about domestic-cat vision, hearing, cognition,
hunting, play, enrichment, stress, health, age, and individual variation can
responsibly constrain how Catflix curates and plays content? `[Research
review, Review question]`

### How the search was run

Searches ran between 28 and 29 July 2026 and covered records published or
indexed by 29 July 2026. The sources searched were PubMed, Crossref,
OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, ScienceDirect and other journal landing pages,
plus backward and forward citation trails (following a paper's own
references, and the papers that later cited it) from the direct display,
color, temporal-modulation, music, play, and stress studies. Semantic
Scholar's public API returned a rate-limit response during the
reproducibility check, so its indexed web records were searched and
inspected directly instead. OpenAlex supplemented bibliographic discovery;
it wasn't part of the originally planned minimum set of databases, but it
helped catch title and DOI variants `[Research review, Search process]`.

#### Search query details (kept for reproducibility)

Query families combined `cat`, `cats`, `domestic cat`, `Felis catus`, or
`Felis silvestris catus` with:

1. `color vision`, `colour discrimination`, `spectral sensitivity`,
   `luminance`, `contrast`;
2. `visual acuity`, `spatial frequency`, `temporal modulation`, `flicker`,
   `motion perception`;
3. `trajectory`, `object permanence`, `occlusion`, `visual cognition`,
   `chasing`;
4. `hearing range`, `sound localization`, `vocal recognition`,
   `audiovisual`, `species appropriate music`;
5. `hunting`, `prey preference`, `object play`, `novelty`, `habituation`,
   `capture`;
6. `television`, `video`, `screen`, `tablet`, `visual enrichment`;
7. `welfare`, `stress`, `frustration`, `shelter`, `habituation`,
   `enrichment`;
8. `aging`, `geriatric`, `cognitive dysfunction`, `health`, `personality`,
   `individual differences`.

Representative database forms were:

```text
PubMed: cats[MeSH Terms] AND (vision OR visual perception OR color vision OR motion perception)
PubMed: cats[MeSH Terms] AND (hearing OR auditory perception OR sound localization OR vocalization)
PubMed: cats[MeSH Terms] AND (play OR hunting OR prey OR enrichment)
Crossref/OpenAlex/Semantic Scholar: "domestic cat" + each query family above
```

(`MeSH Terms` are PubMed's standardized medical subject headings — a
controlled vocabulary used to search consistently across differently worded
articles.)

The direct shelter television paper, the behavioral color-discrimination
paper, the behavioral temporal-modulation paper, and the species-appropriate
music paper were used as seed records: their reference lists and citing
articles were screened for other relevant domestic-cat studies.


### What was included, and how duplicates were removed

Included records were peer-reviewed journal articles studying domestic cats
directly. Behavioral, physiological, clinical, developmental, field, and
review study designs were all eligible. A paper that also covered dogs or
another species alongside domestic cats stayed eligible only when the
feline-specific methods or results could be identified separately. Other cat
species and comparative display studies from other species informed how the
review interpreted things, but weren't counted toward the 60 sources.

Excluded records were books, theses, conference abstracts, preprints without
a published journal version, veterinary-advice web pages, commercial
marketing claims, social media posts, studies about humans watching cats,
and articles where "cat" turned out to mean computed tomography or some
other acronym. Veterinary guidance can support a later clinical review, but
it doesn't replace the peer-reviewed body of evidence used here.

Duplicates were removed using, in order: a normalized DOI (Digital Object
Identifier — a permanent link ID for an academic paper), then PMID (PubMed's
own ID number), then normalized title, year, and study population. Each
paper gets exactly one evidence ID and one primary subject area. The ledger
keeps the DOI and PMID when available. Records with only a DOI were checked
against a journal or indexing landing page; older records without a DOI were
checked against PubMed or a journal's own index. Duplicate publications, and
papers that turned up again under a different search family, were merged
`[Research review, Eligibility and deduplication]`.

### How data was pulled out and judged

For each source, the ledger records: the population studied, sample size,
what stimulus was used, the conditions and design, what behavior or
physiology was measured, the effect found, limitations, how directly it
applies to Catflix, its DOI or PMID, and full citation details. `NR` in a
sample-size field means the number wasn't present in the stable indexed
record the reviewers consulted, and needs the full text recovered before
it's used in any calculation — it does **not** mean the original paper never
reported a sample size at all.

How confident the review is in each piece of evidence is based on: how
directly it applies to household video, whether it's awake behavioral
evidence, whether it's been replicated, sample size, how realistic the test
setting was (ecological validity), what it was compared against, whether the
animal made a real voluntary choice, whether welfare was actually measured,
and the risk of bias in the design. Known weaknesses are named rather than
quietly averaged away: very small trained groups of cats, invasive or
anesthetized physiology studies, the jump from shelter or lab settings to a
home, selection bias from owner reports and online videos, old-fashioned
lab equipment, possible pseudoreplication, and causal claims the study
design can't actually support.

This is a reproducible product literature review, written for a specific
product's decisions — it is **not** a registered systematic review or
meta-analysis, and it does not calculate a single pooled effect size across
studies `[Research review, Data extraction and appraisal]`.

## Glossary

Terms are listed once, in the order they matter most to a first-time reader.

- **Sensory capacity** — what a cat can physically detect or tell apart
  under stated test conditions, regardless of whether it cares.
- **Attention** — looking, orienting, tracking, or approaching, caused by a
  stimulus. Not the same as liking something.
- **Preference** — a voluntary choice between available options, ideally
  shown more than once, with a real chance to walk away.
- **Welfare** — how a cat fares (positively, neutrally, or negatively) over
  time, beyond the moment of looking.
- **Evidence ID** — a study's short label in the evidence ledger, such as
  `VID-01` or `SPA-07`, used to trace a claim back to its source.
- **Evidence ledger** — the spreadsheet that records every source study,
  one row per study, with its population, sample size, method, findings,
  and limitations.
- **Figure-ground separation** — how clearly a subject stands out from its
  background.
- **Luminance contrast** — brightness contrast: how different a subject's
  brightness is from its background's, independent of color.
- **Chromatic contrast** — color contrast: how different a subject's color
  is from its background's, independent of brightness.
- **Visual angle / apparent object size** — how big something looks from a
  given viewing distance, rather than how big it actually is.
- **Critical flicker fusion (CFF)** — the flicker speed at which flickering
  light stops looking like it's flickering and instead looks steady.
- **Judder / cadence errors** — uneven, stuttering motion, often caused by a
  mismatch between how video was recorded and how it's played back.
- **Habituation** — responding less to something the more it's repeated.
- **Novelty family** (`novelty_family` in Catflix's own records) — a clip
  family: a group of clips that share a subject, background, motion, and
  sound, and can be rotated as a set.
- **Occlusion** — passing behind something else, out of sight.
- **Diegetic sound / source-coherent sound** — sound that appears to come
  from the thing shown on screen, rather than from an unrelated source.
- **Ultrasonic** — above the range of human hearing. This is a general
  fact about sound, not a specific finding from any study in this review.
- **Referee notes** — dated, descriptive notes written about one specific
  cat (Arri, Ozzy, or Mika), never a score or an automated profile.
- **Scan sampling** — a research method where an observer checks and
  records what an animal is doing at fixed, repeated intervals, rather than
  watching and recording continuously.
- **Operant discrimination / trained discrimination** — a task where an
  animal is trained to give a specific response (such as pressing a lever
  or making a choice) that shows whether it can tell two things apart.
- **Neurophysiology** — the study of how the nervous system works, often by
  recording electrical activity directly from nerves or the brain.
- **Anesthetized** — sedated and unconscious; used in some physiology
  studies where the animal can't be awake for the measurement.
- **Pseudoreplication** — treating many repeated measurements of very few
  individual animals (or clips) as though they were many independent
  data points, which can make a finding look more solid than it is.
- **Preregistered / preregistration** — a study's design and planned
  analysis written down and filed publicly before any data is collected, so
  the results can't be reshaped afterward to fit what was found.
- **Blinded coding** — having the person who scores behavior in a video do
  so without knowing which condition or group they're looking at, so their
  expectations can't influence the score.
- **Counterbalancing** — systematically varying the order in which
  different conditions are shown to each subject, so the order itself
  doesn't bias the results.
- **Photometry** — measuring light directly with an instrument, rather than
  estimating or describing it by eye.
- **CRT** — cathode-ray tube, an older type of television or monitor
  technology, with different flicker and motion characteristics than modern
  flat-panel screens.
- **NR** — "not reported": in the evidence ledger, this means a number
  (usually a sample size) wasn't present in the indexed record the
  reviewers consulted, and would need the original full text checked before
  being used in any calculation. It does not mean the original study never
  reported the number at all.
